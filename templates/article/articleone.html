How to Calculate Recovery Factor in EOR

What fraction of the original oil in place can you actually produce? Recovery factor is the single most important metric in petroleum engineering — here's how it's calculated and how EOR changes the equation.

What Is Recovery Factor?

Recovery factor is defined as the ratio of recoverable oil to the original oil in place. A recovery factor of 0.35 means 35% of the oil originally in the reservoir will be produced over the life of the field — the remaining 65% stays in the ground, either too costly to produce or physically unreachable with current technology.

Recovery Factor RF = Np / N
SymbolDescriptionUnit
NpCumulative oil producedSTB
NOriginal Oil in Place (OOIP)STB
RFRecovery Factorfraction or %

How Is OOIP Calculated?

Before you can calculate RF, you need to know your OOIP. The volumetric method gives:

N = (7758 × A × h × φ × (1 − Swi)) / Boi
SymbolDescriptionUnit
7758Unit conversion factorbbl/acre-ft
ADrainage areaacres
hNet pay thicknessft
φPorosityfraction
SwiInitial water saturationfraction
BoiInitial oil formation volume factorres bbl/STB

This gives you N in stock-tank barrels. From there, if you know cumulative production or can estimate recoverable reserves, you calculate RF directly. Use the OOIP calculator →

Recovery Factor by Recovery Stage

Stage 1 Primary Recovery 10 – 30% Natural reservoir energy: pressure, solution gas, gravity, water influx
Stage 2 Secondary Recovery 30 – 50% Waterflooding or gas injection; adds 15–25 percentage points
Stage 3 EOR / Tertiary 40 – 70% Chemical, thermal, miscible gas; attacks residual oil saturation Sor

Breaking Down Recovery Factor: The Component Approach

Recovery factor can be decomposed into efficiency terms:

RF = Ev × Ed

Displacement Efficiency

Tells you how completely oil is displaced in areas the flood has contacted. A good waterflood might achieve Ed = 0.60–0.75. EOR methods aim to reduce Sor further, driving Ed toward 0.85–0.95.

Ed = (Soi − Sor) / Soi

Volumetric Sweep Efficiency

Accounts for the fact that injected fluids don't sweep the entire reservoir uniformly. Channeling, fingering, and gravity override all reduce Ev. It is itself a product of areal and invasion sweep:

Ev = Ea × Ei
Key insight: Improving Ev — through well pattern optimisation, mobility control, or conformance treatments — is often where the largest EOR gains are found. Both components must be addressed for meaningful improvement in total RF.

Worked Example

Worked Example Recovery Factor & EOR Scenario

Given: N = 50,000,000 STB  |  Np = 12,500,000 STB  |  Swi = 0.22  |  Sor (waterflood) = 0.28

1

Primary + Secondary RF

RF = 12,500,000 / 50,000,000 = 0.25 (25%)
2

Displacement Efficiency  (Soi = 1 − 0.22 = 0.78)

Ed = (0.78 − 0.28) / 0.78 = 0.641 (64.1%)
3

Implied Volumetric Sweep

Ev = RF / Ed = 0.25 / 0.641 = 0.39 (39%)

Only 39% of the reservoir volume was contacted — a major opportunity for EOR intervention.

4

EOR Scenario — polymer flooding improves Ev to 0.65; Sor drops to 0.18

Ed,EOR = (0.78 − 0.18) / 0.78 = 0.769
RFEOR = 0.65 × 0.769 = 0.50 (50%)
Incremental EOR = 50% − 25% = 25% of OOIP = 12,500,000 STB

Key Takeaways

  • Recovery factor measures what fraction of OOIP is actually produced.
  • Primary recovery rarely exceeds 30%; secondary adds 15–25%; EOR can push totals to 50–70%.
  • RF = Ev × Ed — both components must be addressed for meaningful EOR gains.
  • Displacement efficiency improves when residual oil saturation (Sor) is reduced.
  • Volumetric sweep efficiency improves with better mobility control and conformance.
  • Every percentage point of incremental RF on a large reservoir represents millions of barrels of additional revenue.
