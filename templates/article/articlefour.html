EOR vs Primary Recovery: What's the Difference and Why It Matters

From natural depletion to chemical and thermal flooding — a clear breakdown of the three stages of oil recovery, what drives each one, and when EOR becomes the right choice.

The Three Stages of Oil Recovery

Stage Also Called Mechanism Typical RF
Primary Natural depletion Natural reservoir energy 10 – 30%
Secondary Pressure maintenance Water or gas injection 30 – 50%
EOR / Tertiary Enhanced recovery Chemical, thermal, miscible gas 40 – 70%
Note: These stages are not always sequential. EOR can begin right at the start of production — particularly for heavy oil or very heterogeneous reservoirs where primary recovery would be negligible.

Primary Recovery: Letting the Reservoir Do the Work

Primary recovery relies entirely on the natural energy stored in the reservoir. Several mechanisms can be active:

Most common Solution Gas Drive RF: 15 – 25%

As pressure drops below bubble point, dissolved gas comes out of solution and expands, pushing oil toward the wellbore. As gas breaks out, it becomes the preferential flow path, leaving oil behind.

Structural advantage Gas Cap Drive RF: 20 – 40%

A free gas cap above the oil zone expands downward as reservoir pressure falls, providing efficient displacement. RF depends on cap size and withdrawal rate.

Most efficient natural Natural Water Drive RF: 40 – 50%+

A connected aquifer influxes water as reservoir pressure drops, supporting pressure and displacing oil. When large and responsive, this is the most efficient natural mechanism.

Structural control needed Gravity Drainage RF: varies

In steeply dipping reservoirs, oil drains downward under gravity while gas fills from the top. Efficient at low withdrawal rates with good structural control.

Secondary Recovery: Waterflooding

Waterflooding injects water to maintain pressure and displace oil toward producers. Cheap, widely available, and reasonably effective — a well-designed waterflood adds 15–25% recovery on top of primary. However, water is not oil:

  • Viscous fingering — when water is less viscous than oil (unfavourable mobility ratio M > 1)
  • Gravity override — in thick reservoirs where water sinks below and bypasses upper oil
  • Channeling — through high-permeability streaks that bypass lower-perm zones

Even after a successful waterflood, 30–60% of the original oil remains as residual oil saturation (Sor).

EOR: Breaking Through the Residual Barrier

EOR targets what primary and secondary recovery leave behind. Residual oil is trapped by three mechanisms that conventional flooding cannot overcome:

  • Capillary trapping — interfacial tension between oil and water traps oil in pore throats
  • Viscous bypassing — unfavourable mobility ratios leave oil in less-permeable zones
  • Wettability effects — oil-wet surfaces hold oil tenaciously, reducing displacement efficiency

EOR Methods

Chemical Polymer Flooding
Polymers (HPAM or xanthan gum) increase injection water viscosity, reducing the mobility ratio and improving sweep. Primarily improves Ev rather than Ed.
Mimproved = (krw / (μw + polymer)) / (kro / μo)
Chemical Surfactant & Alkaline Flooding
Surfactants reduce interfacial tension (IFT) between oil and water. At ultra-low IFT (10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴ mN/m), the capillary number Nc exceeds its critical value and residual oil mobilises. Alkalis react with acidic crude components to generate surfactants in situ. Often combined in ASP (Alkaline-Surfactant-Polymer) flooding.
Nc = v × μ / σ
Thermal Steamflooding & SAGD
Heat reduces oil viscosity dramatically — a heavy oil at 20°C with 10,000 cp might drop to 50 cp at 200°C. For heavy oils and tar sands, thermal recovery is often the only viable mechanism. Steam-Assisted Gravity Drainage (SAGD) is the dominant heavy oil recovery method in Canada and Venezuela.
Miscible Gas CO₂ Injection
CO₂ injected above its Minimum Miscibility Pressure (MMP) achieves miscibility with reservoir oil — eliminating the interface between injected gas and oil so that capillary trapping disappears and Sor theoretically goes to zero. CO₂ EOR also offers carbon storage co-benefits.
CO₂ MMP typically: 1,000 – 3,500 psia

Primary vs EOR: Head-to-Head

Factor Primary Recovery EOR
Energy source Natural reservoir energy External (injection, thermal)
Cost Low (drilling + lifting only) High (chemicals, infrastructure)
Recovery factor 10 – 30% Can reach 50 – 70% (cumulative)
Oil targeted Easily mobile oil Residual, viscous, or bypassed oil
Technical complexity Low – Moderate High
Timing of payback Fast Slow (long lead times)

When Is EOR the Right Choice?

  • ✓
    Primary and secondary recovery leave significant oil behind — typical for viscous oils, heterogeneous reservoirs, or oil-wet formations.
  • ✓
    Oil price is high enough — EOR costs $15–50+/bbl in operating costs depending on method; economics must support it.
  • ✓
    Reservoir conditions are suitable — each EOR method has specific requirements (minimum temperature for CO₂ miscibility, permeability for polymer injectivity, etc.).
  • ✓
    Field infrastructure exists — mature fields with existing injection infrastructure reduce EOR project costs significantly.
The future — EOR from day one: An emerging philosophy in reservoir development designs for maximum total recovery from the start, rather than waiting until primary depletion. Especially relevant for heavy oil, tight/unconventional reservoirs, and offshore deepwater fields where re-entry for EOR later is prohibitively expensive.

Key Takeaways

  • Primary recovery uses natural reservoir energy and typically recovers only 10–30% of OOIP.
  • Secondary recovery (waterflooding) adds 15–25% by maintaining pressure and displacing oil.
  • EOR attacks the residual oil left after primary and secondary — through chemical, thermal, or miscible methods.
  • The right EOR method depends on oil viscosity, reservoir heterogeneity, temperature, and economics.
  • EOR is not a last resort — in many cases, it is the primary value driver of a field's total life economics.
