# Material Balance Equation Explained with Examples **Category:** Reservoir Engineering | **Reading Time:** ~9 min | **Level:** Intermediate --- ## Introduction The Material Balance Equation (MBE) is one of the most powerful tools in reservoir engineering. Often called the "tank model" of the reservoir, it is built on a deceptively simple idea: the total expansion of fluids in the reservoir must equal the total production from it (adjusted for any fluid influx or injection). Despite its simplicity in concept, the MBE is elegant in practice — it can be used to estimate OOIP, identify the dominant drive mechanism, predict future performance, and validate simulation models, all without a grid-based numerical simulator. This article explains the MBE from first principles, breaks down each term, and walks through worked examples. --- ## The Core Idea: Conservation of Volume Imagine a reservoir as a closed tank. At initial conditions, that tank is filled with oil, gas, and water at high pressure. When you open a well and produce fluids, the pressure drops. As pressure drops, the remaining fluids expand to fill the void left by what was produced. The MBE simply states: > **Volume of fluids produced = Volume of expansion of remaining reservoir fluids + Volume of fluid influx + Volume of injection** That's it. Every term in the MBE maps to one of these physical phenomena. --- ## The General MBE (Havlena-Odeh Form) The full general MBE for an undersaturated or solution-gas-drive reservoir (Craft, Hawkins & Terry form): ``` N × (Eo + m × Eg + Efw) + We + Winj × Bw + Ginj × Bg = Np × (Bo + (Rp − Rs) × Bg) + Wp × Bw ``` This can be rewritten in compact Havlena-Odeh notation: ``` F = N × Et + We ``` Where: - **F** = Total underground withdrawal (production side) - **N** = Original Oil in Place (STB) - **Et** = Total expansion term - **We** = Cumulative water influx from aquifer Let's define each production and expansion term carefully. --- ## Defining the Terms ### Production Side — Underground Withdrawals (F) ``` F = Np × [Bo + (Rp − Rs) × Bg] + Wp × Bw ``` | Symbol | Meaning | Units | |--------|---------|-------| | Np | Cumulative oil produced | STB | | Bo | Oil formation volume factor | res bbl/STB | | Rp | Cumulative produced GOR | SCF/STB | | Rs | Solution GOR | SCF/STB | | Bg | Gas FVF | res bbl/SCF | | Wp | Cumulative water produced | STB | | Bw | Water FVF | res bbl/STB | ### Expansion Side **Oil and dissolved gas expansion (Eo):** ``` Eo = (Bo − Boi) + (Rsi − Rs) × Bg ``` This captures how oil swells (Bo > Boi) and how dissolved gas is liberated as pressure falls below bubble point. **Gas cap expansion (Eg):** ``` Eg = Boi × [(Bg / Bgi) − 1] ``` If a gas cap exists, it expands as pressure drops, driving additional production. **Connate water and rock compressibility (Efw):** ``` Efw = (1 + m) × Boi × [(cw × Swi + cf) / (1 − Swi)] × ΔP ``` Where: - **m** = Ratio of initial gas cap volume to initial oil zone volume - **cw** = Water compressibility - **cf** = Formation (pore) compressibility - **Swi** = Initial water saturation - **ΔP** = Pressure drop from initial pressure **Total expansion:** ``` Et = Eo + m × Eg + Efw ``` --- ## Simplified Forms for Common Drive Mechanisms The full MBE simplifies depending on the dominant drive. Recognizing which terms dominate helps diagnose reservoir behavior. ### 1. Volumetric Undersaturated Reservoir (P > Pb, no gas cap, no water influx) Below bubble point has not been reached. Only rock and fluid compressibility matter: ``` N = Np × Bo / [(Bo − Boi) + Boi × (cw × Swi + cf) / (1 − Swi) × ΔP] ``` At early life, this simplifies further to: ``` N ≈ Np × Bo / [Boi × ct × ΔP] ``` Where ct = co + cw × Swi + cf (total compressibility). ### 2. Solution Gas Drive (P < Pb, no gas cap, no water influx) The most common drive mechanism for reservoirs without a gas cap or active aquifer: ``` N = F / Eo ``` This is the simplest useful form. Plot F vs Eo — if the relationship is linear through the origin, solution gas drive is confirmed and the slope gives N. ### 3. Gas Cap Drive (m > 0, no water influx) ``` N = F / (Eo + m × Eg) ``` m must be estimated from log data or material balance history matching. ### 4. Water Drive (active aquifer) ``` N × Et + We = F ``` The challenge here is that We is unknown and must be modeled (van Everdingen-Hurst, Fetkovich, etc.). --- ## Havlena-Odeh Straight-Line Method Havlena and Odeh (1963) transformed the MBE into a linear form that makes OOIP estimation and drive mechanism identification visual and intuitive. **For solution gas drive:** ``` F = N × Eo ``` Plot F (y-axis) vs Eo (x-axis). A straight line through the origin with slope N confirms solution gas drive and directly gives OOIP. **If the plot curves upward**, a gas cap or water influx is active. Adding m × Eg or a We term linearizes it again, confirming the drive. **For gas cap + solution gas drive:** ``` F / Eo = N + N × m × (Eg / Eo) ``` Plot F/Eo vs Eg/Eo → straight line with y-intercept = N and slope = N × m. --- ## Worked Example: Solution Gas Drive Reservoir **Reservoir data (at current conditions):** | Parameter | Value | |-----------|-------| | Boi | 1.350 res bbl/STB | | Bo | 1.412 res bbl/STB | | Rsi | 540 SCF/STB | | Rs | 480 SCF/STB | | Bg | 0.00210 res bbl/SCF | | Np | 1,850,000 STB | | Rp | 620 SCF/STB | | Wp | 0 STB (no water production) | **Step 1: Calculate underground withdrawal (F)** ``` F = Np × [Bo + (Rp − Rs) × Bg] F = 1,850,000 × [1.412 + (620 − 480) × 0.00210] F = 1,850,000 × [1.412 + 0.294] F = 1,850,000 × 1.706 F = 3,156,100 res bbl ``` **Step 2: Calculate oil expansion (Eo)** ``` Eo = (Bo − Boi) + (Rsi − Rs) × Bg Eo = (1.412 − 1.350) + (540 − 480) × 0.00210 Eo = 0.062 + 0.126 Eo = 0.188 res bbl/STB (assuming no gas cap or water) ``` Wait — Rsi > Rs means gas is coming out of solution, which is an expansion. Let's correct: ``` Eo = (Bo − Boi) + (Rsi − Rs) × Bg = (1.412 − 1.350) + (540 − 480) × 0.00210 = 0.062 + 0.0630 = 0.1250 res bbl/STB ``` **Step 3: Estimate OOIP** ``` N = F / Eo = 3,156,100 / 0.1250 = 25,249,000 STB ≈ 25.25 MMSTB ``` **Interpretation:** The reservoir has an OOIP of approximately 25.25 million STB. With 1.85 MMSTB produced, the current recovery factor is: ``` RF = 1,850,000 / 25,250,000 = 7.3% ``` Early stage. Significant production potential remains. --- ## Common Mistakes When Applying the MBE **1. Using surface volumes instead of reservoir volumes** Always convert production to reservoir conditions using Bo, Bg, and Bw. Surface and reservoir volumes differ significantly. **2. Ignoring rock compressibility in tight reservoirs** In low-permeability or overpressured reservoirs, cf can be large enough to affect the balance materially. **3. Assuming no water influx too early** An active aquifer can mask solution gas drive on a Havlena-Odeh plot if We is not accounted for — the F vs Eo plot will curve upward, appearing to show influx-free behavior at first, then diverging. **4. Using average reservoir pressure incorrectly** The MBE requires average reservoir pressure, not wellbore pressure. Pressure buildup tests or reservoir simulators are needed to obtain accurate average pressures. --- ## Why the MBE Matters The MBE is not just a textbook exercise: - It gives an independent OOIP estimate that can be compared to volumetric calculations. - It confirms or identifies the dominant drive mechanism, which determines the most appropriate EOR strategy. - It serves as a history-matching tool to calibrate numerical simulators. - It can forecast future production under various pressure depletion scenarios. --- ## Key Takeaways - The MBE is a conservation of volume statement: production = fluid expansion + influx + injection. - The Havlena-Odeh straight-line method linearizes the MBE for easy OOIP estimation and drive identification. - Different drive mechanisms (solution gas, gas cap, water influx) leave identifiable signatures on the Havlena-Odeh plot. - Accurate average reservoir pressure data is essential for reliable MBE results. - Always account for all production streams: oil, free gas, and water. --- ## Further Reading - Havlena, D. and Odeh, A.S.
